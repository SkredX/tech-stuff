LLMs from zero: foundations and the Transformer

Modules 1 and 2, explained in plain words with pictures you can play with. Every section ends with what interviewers actually ask.

Module 1Foundations

1.1 What a language model actually is

A language model has one job: look at the words so far and guess the next one. It doesn't output one answer. It outputs a probability for every possible next word.

Try it below. Click a word to "choose" it. Notice that the chosen word becomes part of the input, and the model guesses again.

Click any word to add it to the sentence. The numbers are made up for illustration.

This loop of predict, add, predict again is called autoregressive generation. That's how ChatGPT writes a whole answer: one token at a time.

Mathematically, the probability of a whole sentence is just the step-by-step guesses multiplied together:

P(w₁, w₂, w₃) = P(w₁) × P(w₂ | w₁) × P(w₃ | w₁, w₂)

Read "|" as "given". So P(w₃ | w₁, w₂) means "the chance of word 3, given words 1 and 2".

RememberAn LLM is just a very large language model: billions of parameters, trained on trillions of tokens. The training goal never changes from "predict the next token". Skills like reasoning and coding appear because of the scale.

1.2 Tokenization: turning text into numbers

A neural network can only do math on numbers. So first we cut the text into pieces called tokens, and give each piece an ID number. The question is: how big should a piece be?

How BPE (Byte Pair Encoding) builds the vocabulary

BPE is the most common subword method. The idea is simple: start with single characters, find the pair that appears together most often, glue it into one new token, and repeat.

Below is a tiny training corpus. The number in brackets is how many times each word appears. _ marks the end of a word. Click Do one merge and watch common chunks like "est" form.

After training, the merges are saved in order. To tokenize new text, the model replays those same merges. Common words end up as one token. Rare words become a few known pieces, so nothing is ever unknown.

Rule of thumb: 1 token ≈ ¾ of an English word, so 100 tokens ≈ 75 words.

Interview trap: "Why can't an LLM count the r's in strawberry?"
Because it never sees letters. It sees tokens like straw + berry. Counting letters means reasoning about something hidden inside the tokens.
Interview trap: "Why does Hindi or Bengali cost more on an API?"
The tokenizer was trained mostly on English text, so other languages get chopped into more tokens per word. More tokens means higher cost, slower answers, and less text fits in the context window.

1.3 Embeddings: turning IDs into meaning

A token ID like 4,821 is just a label. It says nothing about meaning. So each ID is used to look up a row in a big table called the embedding matrix. Its shape is (vocabulary size × d_model), for example 50,000 × 4,096. The row you get back is that token's vector: a list of numbers the model learns during training.

Word2Vec (2013) showed something beautiful: if you train vectors so that words in similar sentences get similar vectors, then meaning turns into geometry. Similar words sit close together, and relationships become directions.

A real embedding has thousands of dimensions; this is a 2D cartoon. Animals cluster together, fruits cluster together, and relationships are parallel arrows.

vector("king") − vector("man") + vector("woman") ≈ vector("queen")

Start at king, subtract the "male" direction, add the "female" direction, and you land next to queen.

Cosine similarity: how we measure "close"

cos θ = (a · b) / (‖a‖ × ‖b‖)

It measures the angle between two vectors and ignores their length. The answer is between −1 and 1.

abcos θMeaning
[1, 2][2, 4]1Same direction: very similar
[1, 0][0, 1]0At right angles: unrelated
[1, 0][−1, 0]−1Opposite directions

You'll use this again in Module 7: RAG systems find relevant documents by comparing embeddings with cosine similarity.

The problem that leads to everything next

"I sat on the river bank."  vs  "I took a loan from the bank."

Word2Vec gives "bank" one fixed vector in both sentences. That's called a static embedding. We need a contextual embedding, where the vector for "bank" changes depending on the words around it. Attention (Module 2) does exactly this.

1.4 Before Transformers: why RNNs lost

Before 2017, text was processed with RNNs (recurrent neural networks). An RNN reads one word at a time and keeps a running "memory" vector. Each step uses the previous memory: h_t = f(h_(t−1), x_t).

Press the buttons and compare how many steps each approach needs.

Press a button.

Three problems, and these are exactly what "why Transformers over RNNs?" is asking for:

ProblemWhat it means in plain words
1. SequentialWord 50 can't be processed until word 49 is done. GPUs are good at doing many things at once, and RNNs can't use that, so training is slow.
2. Memory bottleneckEverything read so far gets squeezed into one fixed-size vector. Like summarizing a whole book into one sentence, early details fade.
3. Vanishing gradientsDuring training, the learning signal gets multiplied at every step and shrinks toward zero. So the model struggles to learn links between far-apart words.

LSTMs add "gates" that decide what to keep and what to forget, which helps with problem 3. But they're still sequential.

The fix: let every word look directly at every other word, all at the same time. That's attention. The 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need removed recurrence completely and introduced the Transformer.

Check yourself: what are the three things that go wrong with RNNs?They're sequential (slow, no parallelism), they squeeze all history into one vector (early information fades), and gradients vanish over long sequences (hard to learn long-range links).

Module 2Attention and the Transformer

2.1 The idea: Query, Key, Value

Think of searching a library:

NameLibrary versionIn the model
Query (Q)What I'm looking for"What kind of information do I need?"
Key (K)The label on each book's spine"Here's what I contain"
Value (V)What's written inside the book"Here's the information I'll hand over"

Every token makes all three by multiplying its embedding by three learned weight matrices: Q = X·W_Q, K = X·W_K, V = X·W_V. Then each token compares its query with every token's key. Good matches get a high score.

Example sentence: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired." To understand "it", the model must figure out what "it" refers to:

Query from "it"

"I'm a pronoun. Which noun am I talking about?"

Keys (match score)

animal: "I'm a living noun" high
street: "I'm a place noun" low
tired: "I'm a feeling" medium

Result

The new vector for "it" is mostly built from the value of "animal", with a bit of "tired".
Now "it" carries the meaning "the tired animal". That's a contextual embedding.

Worked example with real numbers

Let d_k = 2. Query for "it": q = [1, 0]. Keys: animal [2, 0], street [0, 1], tired [1, 1].

Step 1, dot products. q·animal = 1×2 + 0×0 = 2. q·street = 1×0 + 0×1 = 0. q·tired = 1×1 + 0×1 = 1. Scores: [2, 0, 1].
Step 2, scale. √2 ≈ 1.41. Divide: [2/1.41, 0/1.41, 1/1.41] = [1.41, 0, 0.71].
Step 3, softmax. e^1.41 = 4.10, e^0 = 1.00, e^0.71 = 2.03. Total = 7.13. Divide each by the total: [0.58, 0.14, 0.28]. They add up to 1.
Step 4, mix the values. output = 0.58 × v_animal + 0.14 × v_street + 0.28 × v_tired. So "it" becomes mostly "animal", a little "tired", barely "street".

2.3 Why divide by √d_k? (asked a lot)

If the numbers inside q and k are random with variance 1, their dot product adds up d_k such products, so its variance grows to d_k. With d_k = 128, scores can easily be ±20.

Softmax with big numbers becomes "winner takes all": one weight ≈ 100%, everything else ≈ 0%. When that happens, the gradients become tiny, and the model stops learning. Dividing by √d_k brings the spread back to about 1.

2.4 Seeing attention, and the causal mask

Click any word to make it the query and see where it looks. Then turn on the causal mask.

Hand-picked weights to show the idea, not from a real model.

GPT-style models write left to right, so a word must not peek at words that come after it. Before the softmax, we set every "future" score to −∞. Since e^(−∞) = 0, those words get zero attention.

Rows = the word doing the looking. Columns = the word being looked at. Green = allowed. Gray = blocked (score set to −∞). It's a lower-triangle pattern.

Why this matters for training speedWith the mask, one forward pass trains the model to predict the next word at every position at once, and it can't cheat by seeing the answer. A 1,000-word sentence gives 1,000 training examples in one go. RNNs can't do that.

2.5 Multi-head attention

One attention calculation can only focus on one kind of relationship at a time. So we run several heads side by side, each with its own W_Q, W_K, W_V. Each head is smaller: d_k = d_model / h. At the end, all heads' outputs are joined together (concatenated) and mixed by one more matrix, W_O.

Here are three made-up heads looking at the same sentence. Darker = more attention. Rows are the looking word, columns are the word being looked at.

Interview detail: multi-head attention costs about the same as one big head, because each head is smaller. You get several "points of view" for free.

2.6 The Transformer block

This is the decoder-only design used by GPT and Llama. The block in the dashed box is repeated N times (for example, 32 or 80 times).

Important: attention ignores word order. If you shuffle the input words, each word gets the same output, just in a shuffled spot. So "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would look the same. That's why position information must be added at the start. Module 3 covers how (sinusoidal, learned, and RoPE).

2.7 The original encoder–decoder

The 2017 Transformer was built for translation and had two halves.

In cross-attention, the decoder asks the questions (queries) and the encoder's output provides the answers (keys and values). That's how the French output "looks at" the English input.

Later models kept only one half: BERT kept the encoder, GPT kept the decoder, and T5 kept both. Module 3 compares them.

2.8 The cost of attention

Every token scores every other token, so the score table is n × n. Time grows as O(n²·d) and memory as O(n²). Drag the slider to feel how fast that grows.

2.9 Coding question: attention in NumPy

A common interview task is to write scaled dot-product attention from scratch.

import numpy as np

def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    x = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)   # subtract max so exp() can't overflow
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def attention(Q, K, V, causal=False):
    d_k = Q.shape[-1]
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)            # (n, n)
    if causal:
        n = scores.shape[0]
        mask = np.triu(np.ones((n, n), dtype=bool), k=1)   # True above the diagonal
        scores = np.where(mask, -np.inf, scores)
    weights = softmax(scores, axis=-1)         # each row sums to 1
    return weights @ V, weights
What the interviewer checks: you divide by √d_k, you apply softmax along the last axis (each row), and you subtract the max before exp() for numerical stability. Bonus: mention that np.triu(..., k=1) builds the causal mask.
Check yourself: with no positional information, what happens to "dog bites man" vs "man bites dog"?The model sees the same set of words, and attention treats them as an unordered bag. Each word would get exactly the same output vector in both sentences, so the model couldn't tell who bit whom. That's why position must be added (Module 3).

ReviseInterview answers

"Explain the Transformer in 60 seconds"

Text is split into tokens, turned into embedding vectors, and position information is added. Then the vectors go through a stack of identical blocks. In each block, masked multi-head self-attention lets every token gather information from relevant earlier tokens using softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)·V, and then a feed-forward network processes each token on its own. Residual connections and layer norm keep the deep stack trainable. At the end, a linear layer and softmax give a probability for every possible next token. Because there's no recurrence, training runs in parallel over the whole sequence.

Quick Q&A (click to reveal)

Why Transformers over RNNs?Parallel training (no waiting for the previous step), direct connections between any two tokens (no squeezing history into one vector), and no vanishing gradients through time.
Why divide by √d_k?Dot products grow with d_k. Large scores make softmax nearly one-hot, which makes gradients tiny. Dividing by √d_k keeps the scores' variance around 1.
What does the causal mask do?Sets scores for future positions to −∞ before softmax, so they get zero weight. It stops the model from cheating and lets it train on every position in one pass.
Why multiple heads?Each head can learn a different relationship (syntax, coreference, nearby words), at about the same total cost as one big head.
Self-attention vs cross-attention?Self-attention: Q, K, V come from the same sequence. Cross-attention: Q comes from the decoder; K and V come from the encoder's output.
Why subword tokenization?Bounded vocabulary size, no unknown words, and related words share pieces (e.g., "learn", "learning", "learned").
Static vs contextual embeddings?Word2Vec gives one fixed vector per word. A Transformer gives each occurrence its own vector based on context, so "bank" differs in "river bank" and "bank loan".
What is the complexity of attention?O(n²·d) time and O(n²) memory in sequence length n. The main obstacle to long context.
What does the FFN do, and why is it big?It processes each token independently after attention. It expands to about 4× the model width and holds roughly two-thirds of the parameters, much of the model's stored knowledge.
Why residual connections?They add the input back to each layer's output, giving gradients a direct path through very deep networks, so training stays stable.
