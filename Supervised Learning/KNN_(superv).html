0What k-NN Is

k-Nearest Neighbours is the most literal idea in machine learning: to label a new point, look at the training points closest to it and copy the majority. No equations are fit, no boundary is learned in advance — the data is the model.

Given a labelled training set, k-NN classifies a query \(x_q\) by finding its \(k\) nearest neighbours in feature space and taking a majority vote of their labels. For a numeric target it averages instead. "Tell me your neighbours, and I'll tell you who you are."

Three words that define k-NN
  • Supervised — it uses labelled examples, for either classification (vote) or regression (average).
  • Non-parametric — it assumes no functional form for the boundary and has no fixed parameter count; model complexity grows with the data itself.
  • Instance-based / "lazy" — there is no real training phase. It simply stores the training set and defers all computation to query time, in contrast to "eager" learners (SVM, trees, LDA) that fit a model up front.
The one assumption

k-NN works whenever nearby points tend to share labels — the locality (smoothness) assumption. Everything else — which distance, which \(k\), how to scale features — is about making "nearby" mean the right thing.

1The Algorithm, Step by Step

The whole method is three steps: measure distance to everything, keep the closest \(k\), and pool their answers. The only subtlety is what to do when the vote ties.

Given a training set \(D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}\), a distance \(d(\cdot,\cdot)\), a neighbour count \(k\), and a query \(x_q\):

k-NN in pseudocode
  1. Distances. Compute \(d(x_q,x_i)\) for every training point \(i=1\dots N\).
  2. Select. Take the \(k\) points with the smallest distances — call them \(N_k(x_q)\).
  3. Aggregate.
    • Classification: \(\hat y=\operatorname{mode}\{\,y_i : x_i\in N_k(x_q)\,\}\) — the majority class.
    • Regression: \(\hat y=\dfrac1k\sum_{x_i\in N_k(x_q)} y_i\) — the mean target.
Breaking ties

A vote can tie (e.g. \(k=4\) splitting 2–2). Standard fixes: use an odd \(k\) for two classes; shrink \(k\) until the tie breaks; defer to the single nearest neighbour among the tied classes; or weight votes by distance (Section 7). "Training" here is \(O(1)\) — just storing the data — so all the real cost lands at prediction time (Section 9).

2Distance Metrics

"Nearest" is only defined once you pick a distance. The metric is a genuine modelling choice — it reshapes every neighbourhood and can change the prediction.

The usual suspects

Euclidean (\(L_2\)) — straight-line distance, the default:

$$ d(x,z)=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{j}(x_j-z_j)^2}. $$

Manhattan (\(L_1\)) — city-block distance, gentler on large single-feature gaps:

$$ d(x,z)=\textstyle\sum_{j}|x_j-z_j|. $$

Minkowski (\(L_p\)) — the family that contains both:

$$ d(x,z)=\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_{j}|x_j-z_j|^{p}\Bigr)^{1/p}, $$

with \(p=1\) Manhattan, \(p=2\) Euclidean, and \(p\to\infty\) the Chebyshev (\(L_\infty\)) distance \(\max_j|x_j-z_j|\). Other common metrics: Hamming (count of differing attributes, for categorical/binary data), cosine distance (angle-based, for high-dimensional sparse text), and Mahalanobis \(\sqrt{(x-z)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-z)}\), which folds in feature scales and correlations.

3Why Feature Scaling Is Essential

This is the mistake that quietly wrecks k-NN. Because distance sums over features, any feature with a large numeric range dominates the sum — and the others are effectively ignored. Scale first, always.

Two standard fixes put features on comparable footing: min–max normalisation \(x'=\dfrac{x-\min}{\max-\min}\in[0,1]\), or standardisation (z-score) \(x'=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) (mean \(0\), std \(1\)).

Worked example — scaling flips the nearest neighbour

Query \(Q=(\text{salary }50000,\ \text{age }30)\), and two candidates \(P=(52000,45)\), \(R=(60000,28)\). With raw values (salary in dollars):

$$ d(Q,P)=\sqrt{2000^2+15^2}\approx 2000,\qquad d(Q,R)=\sqrt{10000^2+2^2}\approx 10000. $$

So \(P\) looks nearest — but only because the salary term (\(\sim\)millions) drowns out age entirely. Standardise with \(\sigma_{\text{salary}}\approx15000,\ \sigma_{\text{age}}\approx12\):

$$ d(Q,P)=\sqrt{0.13^2+1.25^2}\approx 1.26,\qquad d(Q,R)=\sqrt{0.67^2+0.17^2}\approx 0.69. $$

Now \(R\) is nearest — the 15-year age gap to \(P\) finally counts. The neighbour, and possibly the prediction, flipped purely because of scaling.

Related trap: irrelevant features

Every feature you include adds its gap into the distance — even useless ones. Irrelevant or noisy features dilute the signal from the good ones, so feature selection (or feature weighting) matters for k-NN, and the problem compounds in high dimensions (Section 9).

The flip-flop isn't a bug — it's precisely why \(k\) must be chosen with care (Section 5).

5Choosing k & the Bias–Variance Trade-off

\(k\) is the one knob that matters most. It slides k-NN between memorising every point and ignoring the query entirely — the bias–variance trade-off in its purest form.

Small k vs large k
  • Small \(k\) (e.g. \(k=1\)): highly flexible, follows every point — low bias, high variance. Sensitive to noise and outliers, jagged boundary, prone to overfitting. (1-NN always has zero training error — it is its own nearest neighbour.)
  • Large \(k\): averages over a big neighbourhood — high bias, low variance. Smoother boundary but blurs local structure. At the extreme \(k=N\), every query gets the global majority class, ignoring \(x\) completely.

Increasing \(k\) = more smoothing. A useful mental model (ESL): the effective number of parameters is about \(N/k\).

How to pick \(k\) in practice
  • Cross-validation is the principled answer: sweep a range of \(k\), pick the one with lowest validation error. The error-vs-\(k\) curve is typically U-shaped — too small overfits, too large underfits.
  • Rule of thumb: start near \(k\approx\sqrt{N}\).
  • Use an odd \(k\) for two-class problems to avoid tie votes.

6The Decision Boundary & Voronoi Cells

k-NN never writes down a boundary, yet one exists implicitly. For \(1\)-NN it's a beautiful object: the Voronoi tessellation of the training points.

Under \(1\)-NN, every training point owns the region of space that is closer to it than to any other point — its Voronoi cell. The cell walls are perpendicular bisectors between neighbouring points. The decision boundary is just the union of those walls that separate differently-labelled points, so it is always piecewise-linear.

7Weighted k-NN & the KDE Link

Plain k-NN gives every neighbour an equal vote — but a point right next to the query surely deserves more say than one at the edge of the neighbourhood. Distance-weighting fixes that, and reveals k-NN's kinship with kernel density estimation.

Weight each neighbour by a decreasing function of its distance — \(w_i=\tfrac{1}{d}\), \(\tfrac{1}{d^2}\), or a Gaussian \(w_i=e^{-d^2/2\sigma^2}\) — then vote or average with those weights:

$$ \text{classify: } \hat y=\arg\max_c\!\!\sum_{i\in N_k,\,y_i=c}\!\!w_i, \qquad \text{regress: } \hat y=\frac{\sum_{i\in N_k} w_i\,y_i}{\sum_{i\in N_k} w_i}. $$
Two faces of the same coin — k-NN and KDE

This connects directly to the kernel density estimation in your notes. Both estimate the local density of points; they just hold different things fixed. KDE / Parzen windows fix the window width \(h\) and count how many points fall inside. k-NN fixes the count \(k\) and grows the window until it contains them. Fix the volume, or fix the count — Bishop presents them side by side as the two natural non-parametric density estimators, and weighted k-NN with a kernel sits squarely between them.

8k-NN for Regression

Swap the vote for an average and k-NN predicts numbers instead of classes. Everything else — distances, scaling, choosing \(k\) — carries over unchanged.

For a numeric target, predict the mean of the \(k\) neighbours' values, \(\hat y=\tfrac1k\sum_{i\in N_k}y_i\), or the distance-weighted mean from Section 7. Uniform averaging gives a piecewise-constant surface (the prediction only jumps when the neighbour set changes); weighting smooths it.

Worked example — uniform vs weighted average

A query's three nearest neighbours have target values \(\{10,12,20\}\) at distances \(\{1,2,4\}\). Uniform \(k=3\) prediction:

$$ \hat y=\tfrac13(10+12+20)=14.0. $$

Distance-weighted with \(w_i=1/d_i\) (so \(w=1,\,0.5,\,0.25\), summing to \(1.75\)):

$$ \hat y=\frac{1(10)+0.5(12)+0.25(20)}{1.75}=\frac{21}{1.75}=12.0. $$

Weighting pulls the estimate toward the two close neighbours (\(10,12\)) and discounts the distant outlier (\(20\)): \(12.0\) rather than \(14.0\).

9Cost, Speed & the Curse of Dimensionality

Laziness has a price. k-NN trains instantly but pays at every query, must store all the data, and — most importantly — degrades badly as the number of features grows.

Where the cost lives
  • Training: \(O(1)\) — just store the data. (Memory: \(O(Nd)\), the whole dataset.)
  • Prediction (brute force): \(O(Nd)\) to compute all distances, plus \(O(N)\) to find the \(k\) smallest — per query. That doesn't scale.
  • Speed-ups: space-partitioning trees (k-d trees, ball trees) give \(\sim O(\log N)\) queries in low dimensions; approximate NN (LSH, HNSW graphs) goes sub-linear at scale; and prototype reduction (condensed/edited NN) shrinks the stored set.

The deeper problem is dimensionality. In high \(D\), volume grows so fast that to capture even a small fraction of the data in a neighbourhood you must span almost the entire range of every feature — so "nearby" points aren't actually near.

Distance concentration

The same effect shows up as concentration: in high dimensions the nearest and farthest points sit at almost the same distance (\(d_{\max}/d_{\min}\to 1\)), so neighbour rankings get swamped by noise. The remedy is to reduce dimensionality first — PCA or LDA (see the companion guide) — or to select/weight features and learn a better metric.

Interview questions

Why is k-NN called "lazy" and "non-parametric"?

Lazy: it does no work at training time beyond storing the data — all computation is deferred to the query. Non-parametric: it assumes no functional form for the boundary and has no fixed parameter count; the model is the data, and its complexity grows with \(N\).

How does \(k\) trade off bias and variance?

Small \(k\) is flexible — low bias, high variance, jagged boundary, sensitive to noise. Large \(k\) averages more — high bias, low variance, smoother boundary. At \(k=N\) every query returns the global majority class.

How do you choose \(k\)?

By cross-validation: sweep \(k\) and take the lowest validation error (the curve is usually U-shaped). A common starting heuristic is \(k\approx\sqrt N\); use an odd \(k\) for two-class problems to avoid ties.

Why must you scale the features?

Distance sums over features, so a large-range feature dominates and the rest are ignored. Min–max normalisation or z-score standardisation puts every feature on comparable footing — without it the nearest neighbour can be decided by a single feature's units.

Which distance metric, and when?

Euclidean (\(L_2\)) by default; Manhattan (\(L_1\)) when you want robustness to large single-feature gaps or grid-like data; Minkowski to interpolate; Hamming for categorical/binary; cosine for high-dimensional sparse text; Mahalanobis when features are correlated or on different scales.

What is the curse of dimensionality for k-NN?

In high dimensions, points spread out and distances concentrate (\(d_{\max}/d_{\min}\to1\)), so neighbourhoods stop being local and "nearest" becomes meaningless. You'd need exponentially more data. Mitigate with dimensionality reduction (PCA/LDA), feature selection, or a learned metric.

How do you make k-NN fast at query time?

Brute force is \(O(Nd)\) per query. Use space-partitioning trees (k-d, ball trees) for low dimensions, approximate nearest-neighbour methods (LSH, HNSW) at scale, and prototype-reduction (condensed/edited NN) to shrink the stored set.

What shape is the k-NN decision boundary?

Piecewise-linear and entirely data-defined. For \(1\)-NN it's exactly the boundary of the Voronoi tessellation of the training points; larger \(k\) yields a smoother, simpler version. Because it's non-parametric, the boundary can be arbitrarily complex.

Is there any theoretical guarantee?

Yes — the Cover–Hart result: as \(N\to\infty\), the error rate of \(1\)-NN is at most twice the optimal Bayes error rate. Remarkably good for so simple a rule.

Last-minute gotchas
  • Scale your features — the number-one k-NN mistake.
  • Choose \(k\) by cross-validation, not by guessing; use odd \(k\) to avoid ties.
  • High dimensions break k-NN — reduce dimensionality first.
  • Class imbalance biases the vote toward the majority class (it floods every neighbourhood) — use distance weighting or rebalance.
  • Irrelevant features add noise to every distance — select or weight features.
  • It stores the whole dataset — memory- and latency-heavy at prediction time.
