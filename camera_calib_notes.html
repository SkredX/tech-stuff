The one-sentence idea

A camera converts 3D world coordinates into 2D image pixels. Camera calibration reverses-engineers that conversion: given many known 3D points and their pixel locations, recover the camera's internal geometry (intrinsics: focal length, principal point) and external pose (extrinsics: rotation and translation). This lab does it from scratch using only SVD and RQ decomposition — no OpenCV calibration functions allowed.

1The Big Picture — What Calibration Does & Why

Every measurement made from a camera image depends on knowing the camera's geometry. Calibration recovers it — once — so you can use it for everything else.

Why a checkerboard? Why multiple distances?

Checkerboard: the corners of a checkerboard grid are precisely locatable (sub-pixel accuracy) and form a known, regular 3D pattern. Their coordinates in the physical world are exactly (i × square_size, j × square_size, Z) — no measurement uncertainty.

Multiple Z-planes: a single image from one distance leaves the system underdetermined or degenerate. Seeing the same pattern at 9 different Z-distances (400mm to 1200mm) gives enough independent equations to solve for all 12 unknowns of M robustly.

2The Pinhole Camera Model — Perspective Projection

The foundational model for any camera: light from a 3D point passes through a single point (the optical centre) and lands on the image plane. This geometry produces the perspective projection equations — the basis of everything else in this lab.

From similar triangles to the full projection equations

From the geometry, a world point at position (X, Y, Z) in camera coordinates projects to image coordinates:

$$x = f\cdot\frac{X}{Z}, \qquad y = f\cdot\frac{Y}{Z}$$

But the image coordinate origin is usually at the top-left corner of the image, not the centre. Adding the principal point offset (c_x, c_y) (the pixel where the optical axis hits the image):

$$u = f_x\cdot\frac{X}{Z} + c_x, \qquad v = f_y\cdot\frac{Y}{Z} + c_y$$

where \(f_x = f/s_x\) and \(f_y = f/s_y\) are the focal length in pixel units (f in mm, divided by pixel pitch in mm/pixel). These are the two key equations that the entire calibration procedure is trying to recover.

What "perspective" means — why distant objects look smaller

Notice the Z in the denominator: the image coordinate x = f·X/Z. Double the distance Z → image size halves. This is exactly perspective: objects shrink with distance. A 25mm checkerboard square at Z=400mm appears \(f \times 25/400 \approx 89\) pixels wide (with f≈1422). At Z=1200mm the same square appears only \(1422 \times 25/1200 \approx 30\) pixels wide. This is why the lab uses 9 images at different distances — the scale variation provides rich information for calibration.

3Homogeneous Coordinates & Projective Geometry

Perspective projection involves division by Z, which makes it non-linear. Homogeneous coordinates elegantly turn this division into a linear matrix multiplication — the key move that makes calibration tractable.

The Problem with Division

The equation \(u = f_x X/Z + c_x\) has Z in the denominator — it's not a linear transformation. Linear algebra loves linear transformations (matrices). So we need to represent this projection as a matrix multiply.

Homogeneous coordinates — adding a scale factor

A 2D point \((u, v)\) becomes the homogeneous 3-vector \((u, v, 1)^T\). A 3D point \((X,Y,Z)\) becomes the 4-vector \((X,Y,Z,1)^T\). In homogeneous coordinates, a scale factor doesn't change which point you're representing: \((u,v,1) \sim (ku, kv, k)\) for any \(k \neq 0\).

The projection \(u = f_x X/Z + c_x\) can now be written as a matrix multiplication followed by a single division to un-homogenise:

$$\begin{pmatrix}su\\sv\\s\end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}f_x & 0 & c_x & 0 \\ 0 & f_y & c_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}}_{M \text{ (3×4)}}\begin{pmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{pmatrix}$$

Then \(u = (su)/s\) and \(v = (sv)/s\) where \(s = Z\). This is the magic: the messy division has been absorbed into the homogeneous representation — everything above the line is a clean matrix multiply.

4The Projection Matrix M = K[R|t]

The 3×4 projection matrix M is the star of this lab — everything builds up to estimating it. Its structure M = K[R|t] encodes both the camera's internal geometry and its position in the world.

The exact relationship: how M maps a world point to a pixel

Writing it out fully: the world point \(\mathbf{P}_w = (X_w, Y_w, Z_w, 1)^T\) is first transformed into camera coordinates by \([R|t]\), then projected by K:

$$s\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{pmatrix} = K\left[R \;\Big|\; t\right]\begin{pmatrix}X_w\\Y_w\\Z_w\\1\end{pmatrix} = K\underbrace{\left(R\begin{pmatrix}X_w\\Y_w\\Z_w\end{pmatrix}+t\right)}_{\text{camera coords }(X_c,Y_c,Z_c)}$$

This is a chain: world → camera (via R,t) → image (via K) → pixel (divide by Z_c). Each step is handled by part of M.

5Intrinsic Matrix K — All 5 Parameters Explained

K encodes everything about the camera's internal optics and sensor geometry. Five numbers. This is what the lab is trying to recover.

The 5 intrinsic parameters — physical meanings
  • f_x — focal length along the x-axis in pixel units. Equals physical focal length f divided by horizontal pixel pitch s_x (mm/pixel). Lab result: 1422 pixels.
  • f_y — focal length along the y-axis in pixel units. Usually very close to f_x unless pixels are non-square. Lab result: 1421 pixels (nearly identical to f_x — square pixels).
  • c_x — x-coordinate of the principal point (where the optical axis pierces the image plane). Ideally W/2. Lab result: 511.9 ≈ W/2 for a 1024-pixel-wide image.
  • c_y — y-coordinate of the principal point. Ideally H/2. Lab result: 512.3 ≈ H/2.
  • γ (skew) — non-orthogonality of the pixel axes. Zero for virtually all modern cameras. This lab sets γ=0 as an assumption: K_final[0,1] = 0.
Real numbers from this lab — sanity-checking the result

fx = 1422 pixels, fy = 1421 pixels: the camera has a square sensor with very nearly square pixels (ratio fy/fx = 0.9995). The physical focal length can be estimated as f ≈ fx × pixel_pitch. If pixel_pitch ≈ 3.4μm (typical phone sensor), then f ≈ 1422 × 0.0034mm ≈ 4.8mm — reasonable for a compact camera.

cx = 511.9, cy = 512.3: the principal point is essentially at the image centre. For a 1024×1024 image, ideal values are (512, 512). This small offset (0.1 and 0.3 pixels) is within expected manufacturing tolerance — a good sanity check that the calibration worked.

6Extrinsic Parameters R and t

While K describes the camera itself, R and t describe where the camera is in the world — its position and orientation. These change every time you move the camera.

Properties of R and t

R (rotation, 3×3): orthogonal matrix — R^T = R^{-1}, det(R) = +1. Has only 3 degrees of freedom (not 9) — parameterisable as three rotation angles (roll, pitch, yaw). It rotates the world frame to align with the camera frame.

t (translation, 3×1): the position of the world origin expressed in camera coordinates. Note: this is NOT the position of the camera in world coordinates. The camera's world position is -R^T · t.

Together [R|t]: 6 degrees of freedom (3 for rotation, 3 for translation) — the pose of the camera.

7Setting Up the Linear System — DLT Algorithm

The Direct Linear Transform (DLT) turns the non-linear problem of recovering M from point correspondences into a standard Ax=0 linear system. Every line in compute_projection_matrix() builds this system.

From One Point Pair to Two Equations

We know: for world point \(\mathbf{P}=(X,Y,Z,1)^T\) and image point \(\mathbf{p}=(u,v)\):

$$\begin{pmatrix}su\\sv\\s\end{pmatrix} = M\begin{pmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{m}_1^T\mathbf{P}\\\mathbf{m}_2^T\mathbf{P}\\\mathbf{m}_3^T\mathbf{P}\end{pmatrix}$$

where \(\mathbf{m}_1^T, \mathbf{m}_2^T, \mathbf{m}_3^T\) are the three rows of M. Since \(u = (su)/s = (\mathbf{m}_1^T\mathbf{P})/(\mathbf{m}_3^T\mathbf{P})\), cross-multiplying eliminates s:

Deriving the 2 linear equations per point

\(u \cdot \mathbf{m}_3^T\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{m}_1^T\mathbf{P}\) and \(v \cdot \mathbf{m}_3^T\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{m}_2^T\mathbf{P}\)

Stack the 12 elements of M into a vector \(\mathbf{m} = (m_{11}, m_{12}, \ldots, m_{34})^T\). Rearranging:

$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}X & Y & Z & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -uX & -uY & -uZ & -u \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & X & Y & Z & 1 & -vX & -vY & -vZ & -v\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{2 rows of A, for this one point}}\mathbf{m} = \mathbf{0}$$

These are exactly the two rows added to A in the code's inner loop. N point pairs → 2N rows → A is (2N × 12). For this lab: 486 pairs → A is (972 × 12).

Syntax — building the A matrix (from compute_projection_matrix) 
def compute_projection_matrix(P, p):
    N = P.shape[0]   # number of point pairs
    A = []

    for i in range(N):
        X, Y, Z = P[i]   # unpack 3D world point
        u, v = p[i]      # unpack 2D image point

        # Row 1: u equation  [X,Y,Z,1, 0,0,0,0, -u*X,-u*Y,-u*Z,-u]
        A.append([X, Y, Z, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, -u*X, -u*Y, -u*Z, -u])
        # Row 2: v equation  [0,0,0,0, X,Y,Z,1, -v*X,-v*Y,-v*Z,-v]
        A.append([0, 0, 0, 0, X, Y, Z, 1, -v*X, -v*Y, -v*Z, -v])

    A = np.array(A)   # shape: (2N, 12)
    return A

8Solving Am = 0 via SVD

We have a (2N × 12) matrix A and want the non-trivial 12-vector m such that Am ≈ 0. This is a classic least-squares null-space problem — and SVD solves it in one step.

Why the Last Row of V^T

SVD decomposes A = U Σ V^T. The singular values (diagonal of Σ) are ordered largest to smallest. The last row of V^T corresponds to the smallest singular value — meaning A times that vector is as close to zero as any unit-norm vector can make it. That's exactly what we want: the m minimising ‖Am‖.

Syntax — SVD solution for M 
# Solve Am = 0 using SVD
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)   # Full SVD decomposition
# U: (972,972), S: (12,) — singular values, Vt: (12,12)
# Note: np.linalg.svd returns V^T directly (Vt), not V

m = Vt[-1]                    # last row of V^T = null space vector
# Vt[-1] is equivalent to Vt[11] (last of 12 rows)

M = m.reshape(3, 4)            # reshape 12-vector to 3×4 projection matrix

# Sanity check: verify Am ≈ 0 (residual should be tiny)
residual = np.dot(A, m)
print(f"Residual norm: {np.linalg.norm(residual):.6f}")  # should be near 0
np.linalg.svd returns V^T not V — don't confuse them

Many textbooks define SVD as A = UΣV^T and store the columns of V. NumPy's np.linalg.svd returns Vt where each row is an eigenvector (so Vt is V^T). The null-space solution is the last row of Vt (= Vt[-1]), corresponding to the smallest singular value. If you mistakenly use V[:, -1] you'd be accessing the last column of V^T, which is the row — same thing! But Vt[:, -1] would be the last column of V^T, which is wrong.

Why the null space = the solution

The system Am = 0 is homogeneous — any scaling of m (e.g. 2m) is also a solution. Projection matrices are defined up to scale, so this is fine. SVD finds the unit-norm vector m that minimises ‖Am‖. With enough well-conditioned point pairs (this lab uses 486), the smallest singular value is near zero and its corresponding right singular vector is the unique answer in the null space of A.

9RQ Decomposition — Recovering K and R from M

SVD gives us M as a single 3×4 matrix. But we want K and R separately. RQ decomposition is the algebraic tool that factors the 3×3 left part of M into an upper-triangular matrix K times an orthogonal matrix R.

Why RQ not QR?

Standard QR decomposition gives A = Q·R where Q is orthogonal (unitary) and R is upper triangular. But we want A = K·R where K is upper triangular and R is orthogonal — the opposite order. This is called RQ decomposition. SciPy's scipy.linalg.rq(A) gives exactly this: it returns (upper_triangular, orthogonal) satisfying A = rq_R × rq_Q — we use rq_R as K and rq_Q as the rotation R.

Syntax — complete RQ decomposition (from the lab) 
from scipy.linalg import rq

def get_intrinsics_extrinsics(M):
    # Step 1: split M = [A | b]
    A = M[:, 0:3]    # left 3×3 = K·R
    b = M[:, 3]     # rightmost column = K·R·t = K·t_cam

    # Step 2: RQ decompose to get K and R
    K, R = rq(A)    # K: upper triangular, R: orthogonal

    # Step 3: fix signs (focal lengths must be positive)
    T = np.diag(np.sign(np.diag(K)))  # diagonal matrix of signs
    K = np.dot(K, T)   # flip columns of K where needed
    R = np.dot(T, R)   # flip corresponding rows of R

    # Step 4: recover t
    t = np.dot(np.linalg.inv(K), b)

    # Step 5: normalize so K[2,2] = 1 (homogeneous)
    scale = K[2, 2]
    K = K / scale
    t = t / scale

    return K, R, t

# Apply and zero out skew (theta = 90° assumption)
K, R, t = get_intrinsics_extrinsics(M_estimated)
K_final = K.copy()
K_final[0, 1] = 0    # force zero skew since axes are perpendicular
Why the sign fix? — T = diag(sign(diag(K)))

RQ decomposition is not unique — you can flip the sign of any column of K and simultaneously flip the corresponding row of R and get another valid decomposition. The physical constraint is that diagonal elements of K (f_x, f_y, K[2,2]) must be positive (focal lengths are positive). T = np.diag(np.sign(np.diag(K))) creates a diagonal matrix whose entries are +1 where K's diagonal is already positive, and −1 where it's negative. Right-multiplying K by T and left-multiplying R by T flips the signs appropriately while maintaining A = K·R.

10Corner Detection & World Points Setup

Before any linear algebra, you need the raw data: 3D world coordinates and their corresponding 2D pixel coordinates. This section covers exactly how the lab builds both.

Syntax — building world and image point arrays 
CHECKERBOARD_DIMS = (9, 6)   # (cols-1, rows-1) of interior corners
SQUARE_SIZE = 25              # mm per square

# Create the base (X,Y) grid — same for every image
base_objp = np.zeros((CHECKERBOARD_DIMS[0] * CHECKERBOARD_DIMS[1], 3), np.float32)
base_objp[:, :2] = np.mgrid[0:CHECKERBOARD_DIMS[0], 0:CHECKERBOARD_DIMS[1]].T.reshape(-1, 2)
base_objp *= SQUARE_SIZE       # scale from grid indices to mm
# shape: (54, 3) — 54 corners, columns X, Y, Z (Z still 0)

# For each image: copy and set the Z coordinate
current_objp = base_objp.copy()
current_objp[:, 2] = valid_z[idx]   # e.g. 400.0 for image c1

# Detect corners in image (cv2 allowed for this step)
ret, corners = cv2.findChessboardCorners(gray, CHECKERBOARD_DIMS, None)

if ret:
    term = (cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_EPS + cv2.TERM_CRITERIA_MAX_ITER, 30, 0.001)
    corners2 = cv2.cornerSubPix(gray, corners, (11,11), (-1,-1), term)
    img_points.append(corners2.reshape(-1, 2))    # shape: (54, 2)
    obj_points.append(current_objp)               # shape: (54, 3)

# After all images: stack into single arrays
PTS_3D = np.vstack(obj_points)   # shape: (486, 3)
PTS_2D = np.vstack(img_points)   # shape: (486, 2)
Key syntax: np.mgrid[0:9, 0:6].T.reshape(-1, 2)
  • np.mgrid[0:9, 0:6] — creates a grid of shape (2, 9, 6): first array has column indices 0–8 repeated across rows; second has row indices 0–5 repeated across columns.
  • .T — transposes to (6, 9, 2).
  • .reshape(-1, 2) — flattens to (54, 2): 54 corners, each with (col_idx, row_idx). Multiplied by 25mm gives (X, Y) in mm.
cv2.cornerSubPix — why it's there and what it does

cv2.findChessboardCorners finds corners to integer pixel accuracy. cv2.cornerSubPix refines those locations to sub-pixel accuracy using the gradient information in a (11×11) neighbourhood. Even a 0.1-pixel error in the 2D coordinates affects the calibration accuracy — this step is essential for quality results. The termination criteria (TERM_CRITERIA_EPS + TERM_CRITERIA_MAX_ITER, 30, 0.001) means: iterate until change is <0.001 pixels OR 30 iterations, whichever comes first.

M is determined only up to scale — normalisation matters

The DLT gives M as the null-space vector, defined only up to a scalar multiple. Two matrices M and 2M represent the same projection (since we always divide by the third row anyway). The RQ decomposition and normalisation K = K / K[2,2] ensure K is in canonical form with K[2,2]=1 — this is necessary before comparing values between different calibrations or using them in further computations.

Sanity checks — how to know if your calibration is reasonable
  • fx ≈ fy: ratio is 1422/1421 = 1.0007 — essentially equal, confirming square pixels. Large deviation (>5%) suggests non-square pixels or a bug.

  • Projection matrix M: the last row of M (normalised by M[2,3]) encodes the principal ray direction. For a frontal-facing camera, M[2,:3] should be approximately [0,0,1] in camera coordinates.

Interview Q&A

What are the intrinsic parameters of a camera and what do each mean?

f_x, f_y — focal length in pixel units along x and y axes. For square pixels f_x ≈ f_y. Equal to physical focal length divided by pixel pitch. (c_x, c_y) — the principal point: where the optical axis pierces the image plane, ideally at the image centre. γ — skew: non-orthogonality between pixel axes, zero for modern cameras. These are intrinsic (internal to the camera) because they're fixed properties of the lens/sensor — they don't change when you move the camera.

Derive the two DLT equations for one point correspondence.

Start from \(s\tilde{p} = M\tilde{P}\): \(su = \mathbf{m}_1^T\mathbf{P}\), \(sv = \mathbf{m}_2^T\mathbf{P}\), \(s = \mathbf{m}_3^T\mathbf{P}\). Dividing: \(u = \mathbf{m}_1^T\mathbf{P}/\mathbf{m}_3^T\mathbf{P}\). Cross-multiply: \(u\cdot\mathbf{m}_3^T\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{m}_1^T\mathbf{P}\). Rearrange as a linear constraint on the 12 unknowns of M: \([X,Y,Z,1,0,0,0,0,-uX,-uY,-uZ,-u]\mathbf{m}=0\). Similarly for v: \([0,0,0,0,X,Y,Z,1,-vX,-vY,-vZ,-v]\mathbf{m}=0\). These are exactly the two rows added per point in the code.

Why does SVD solve Am = 0, and which singular vector is the answer?

We have 2N equations in 12 unknowns, but the system is homogeneous (Am=0) — any scale of the solution is also a solution. SVD decomposes A = UΣV^T. The right singular vectors (rows of V^T) are ordered by their corresponding singular values, which measure how much A "stretches" each vector. The vector corresponding to the smallest singular value is stretched the least — i.e., A times it is closest to zero — making it the best solution to Am=0. This is Vt[-1] in NumPy's convention (V^T is returned directly).

Why use RQ decomposition to recover K and R? Why not QR?

We have A = KR where K should be upper-triangular (the intrinsic matrix) and R orthogonal (rotation). This is the opposite order from QR (Q orthogonal, R upper-triangular). RQ decomposition gives exactly upper-triangular × orthogonal = K × R. SciPy's rq(A) directly computes this. QR would give the factors in the wrong order and we'd need to invert/transpose to get K and R separately.

What is the sign fix step doing in the RQ decomposition code?

RQ decomposition is not unique — you can flip the sign of any pair (column of K, row of R) and still have A = KR. Physically, focal lengths (the diagonal of K) must be positive. T = np.diag(np.sign(np.diag(K))) creates a diagonal matrix with +1 where the diagonal is already positive, −1 where it's negative. Right-multiplying K by T flips the signs of K's columns; left-multiplying R by T flips the corresponding rows. This preserves A = KR while guaranteeing K's diagonal is positive.

How many unknowns does M have? How many point correspondences are needed minimum?

M has 3×4 = 12 elements, but since it's defined up to scale, effectively 11 degrees of freedom. Each point pair gives 2 equations (u and v). Minimum to solve: 11/2 = 5.5 → need at least 6 point pairs. This lab uses 486 pairs for a highly over-determined, robust solution. More points average out noise — the SVD finds the least-squares solution automatically.

Last-minute gotchas
  • np.linalg.svd(A) returns Vt (V^T), not V. The null-space solution is Vt[-1] (last row), not Vt[:,-1] (last column, which would be wrong).
  • Singular values from np.linalg.svd are in descending order — the smallest is S[-1], corresponding to the last row of Vt.
  • The A matrix in the DLT has a strict structure: the u-row has the last 4 columns as −uX, −uY, −uZ, −u and the v-row has −vX, −vY, −vZ, −v. Mixing these up gives nonsense results.
  • After RQ decomposition, always check np.allclose(K @ R, A) to verify the factorisation is correct before using K and R for anything.
  • Skew is set to zero after RQ decomposition (K_final[0,1] = 0) as a post-processing step, not during — the RQ will still give a non-zero skew estimate which is discarded by assumption.
  • The 9 in CHECKERBOARD_DIMS means 9 interior corners in the horizontal direction — a standard 10×7 physical checkerboard has 9×6 interior corners (outer squares don't create visible interior corners).
  • scipy.linalg.rq vs np.linalg.qr — these are different functions. Make sure to from scipy.linalg import rq and not np.linalg.qr.
